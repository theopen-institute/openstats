Income in Nepal, 2008–2018

  • Week 1 opened with a claim that sounded wrong and turned out true. This week opens with a puzzle from Nepal’s own data, solved on the next slide. The title is deliberately neutral: let them find the puzzle.
  • Set it up. The Nepal Labour Force Survey, 2008 and 2017/18, as tabulated by the ILO (ILOSTAT): average monthly earnings in the main job. Prices roughly doubled between the two surveys (×2.1, about 8% a year), so every rupee figure is converted to 2025/26 rupees with the Nepal Rastra Bank’s consumer price index. The bars are the change after inflation; the rupee amounts are written in them.
  • Who’s counted: paid employees only, people working for a wage or salary. That leaves out most of Nepal’s workers: farmers on their own land, shopkeepers and other self-employed people, and family members working unpaid. In 2008 paid employees were about 2 million people; by 2017/18, about 3.8 million.
  • Two groups, as the ILO’s published tables draw them: secondary education or less, and post-secondary. Each group’s average is built from the ILO’s averages for each education level, weighted by how many employees have it. Both groups’ real earnings rose a lot, 67% and 58%.
  • Ask: what happened to both groups together, all paid employees? Most people will say somewhere between 58% and 67%.
  • Click: 55%. Less than either group. Ask the room how that’s possible. An average of two groups should land between them.
  • Click: the question, on the slide.
  • Someone may get there: the mix changed. The lower-paid group grew from 74% to 86% of employees, which pulls the overall average down. True, but why did it grow so fast? Leave it open: the next slide.
  • A caution if anyone checks the ILO’s website: the ILO also publishes an all-employee average for 2008 (Rs 6,287) that is higher than its own education rows add up to (Rs 5,412). In 2017/18 they match. 2008’s earnings data probably missed some employees, likely rural and less educated ones. Grown from the published total, all employees’ real gain is 34%, not 55%. We build every average here from the same rows, so the groups and the total agree with each other.

The distributions behind the averages

  • The answer, and our first look at this week’s subject: a distribution. Each hump shows how one group’s earnings spread out: how many employees earn each amount. Top, secondary or less; bottom, post-secondary, the same order as the last slide. The area under a hump is the number of employees, on one scale. The thick lines are each group’s average, starting at the last slide’s 2008 numbers: Rs 14,640 and Rs 24,180, with both groups together at Rs 17,070.
  • Say up front that the shapes are made up. The survey tables give each education level’s size and average, not its spread, so each level is drawn as a smooth curve with its real average and an invented spread, and the humps add them up. The sizes and averages are real; the exact shapes aren’t, because the survey’s microdata (which would give them) isn’t publicly available. The footnote says so.
  • Click: ten years pass, and we keep counting the groups the way 2008 did, with grades 11–12 (the old Intermediate and Proficiency Certificate) as post-secondary. Both humps grow (many more paid employees) and move right. Post-secondary’s average reaches Rs 32,350, up 34%; secondary or less reaches Rs 24,330, up 66%; both groups together Rs 26,430, up 55%, in between, as an average should be. No puzzle yet.
  • Click: the green part of the post-secondary hump is grades 11–12: 463,000 employees in 2017/18, almost half the group. Point out where they sit: at the low end of post-secondary, below its average, but just above the secondary group’s average.
  • Click, and let the animation run. The 2017/18 tables count grades 11–12 as secondary. Same people, same pay: watch them rise out of post-secondary and push up the secondary curve from underneath. Post-secondary lost its lowest earners, so its average jumps from Rs 32,350 to Rs 38,260. Secondary or less gained people who earn a bit more than its average, so its average rises too, from Rs 24,330 to Rs 24,500. Both groups together: unchanged. Those are the published 2017/18 numbers from the last slide.
  • This is the Will Rogers paradox, after his joke that when the Okies left Oklahoma for California, they raised the average intelligence of both states. Medicine has a famous version: better scans move patients with small tumours from “early stage” to “late stage”, and survival improves in both stages while no patient lives longer.
  • That solves the puzzle. The published growth compares 2008’s groups, with grades 11–12 counted as post-secondary, to 2017/18’s, with them counted as secondary. Of post-secondary’s published rise of Rs 14,080, Rs 5,910 (42%) is the move. Count both years the same way and there’s no paradox: both groups together land between the two groups.
  • If someone asks why 42% here and “most of it” elsewhere: it depends which year’s grouping you hold fixed. Counting both years the 2017/18 way (degrees only), post-secondary grew just 10% and the move is three quarters of the published rise. Either way, a big share of the published gain is the label.
  • Be honest about the source: the ILO doesn’t document the change. It’s inferred from the ILO’s own detailed counts: grades 11–12 appear as short-cycle tertiary (ISCED 5) only in 2008, and the post-secondary non-tertiary level (ISCED 4) appears only in 2017/18, which fits Nepal’s 2016 education reform making grades 9–12 secondary school.
  • And the real story is the opposite of the published one: degree holders doubled, from 7% to 14% of paid employees, and their real pay barely grew. Economists call it graduate premium compression.
  • Caveats, if asked: these are survey estimates, so differences of a percent or so are noise. Only two survey rounds have earnings by education (the ILO hasn’t tabulated the 1998/99 one). Settling it properly takes the survey’s microdata from the National Statistics Office.
  • Click: the lesson for the week, in one line. Distributions reveal what averages hide. An average summarizes a crowd, a distribution. Change who’s in the crowd and the average changes, even when nobody’s situation does. Before comparing averages, ask who’s in each group, and look at the whole distribution when you can.

You already think in distributions

  • Same move as Week 1’s “You already understand probability”: they already do this, nobody has told them what it’s called.
  • Ask someone in the room how long their trip here takes. Someone will say “usually about 30 minutes,” or “it depends.”
  • Click: “Usually 30 minutes.” That’s not one number. It’s a summary of a lot of trips.
  • Click, first card: “usually” means not every trip. Some take 20 minutes, some 45. How trips vary is a population distribution: many cases, each different.
  • Click, second card: how do you know? You’ve done it a hundred times. Someone who’s done it twice would say “about half an hour, I think,” and could be quite wrong. How sure you are is a certainty distribution.
  • Click, third card: what if it rains? Then the whole spread shifts: everything gets slower, and less predictable. A distribution that moves with something else is a correlative distribution.
  • Click: they already think in all three. This week we draw them, and use them.

  • The top row is three countries with the same most common income (the peak is in the same spot); only the spread changes. A very unequal country: the same peak, a very long tail. Ask: where would the average be in each? The longer the tail, the further the few at the top pull the average above what most people earn.

Describing distributions: center, spread, shape

  • Our running example for the rest of the week (the footnote has the source): the WHO’s STEPS survey of noncommunicable-disease risk factors, run in Nepal in 2019 by the Nepal Health Research Council. Surveyors visited households across all seven provinces, picked one adult aged 15–69 in each, and measured their height and weight. We use the 5,290 aged 18 and over. It’s about people, it’s from here, and height and weight are easy to reason about; the same data carries us from one variable (Part 1) to two (Part 3), and into next week.
  • Read the shape first: one hump, roughly symmetric. The bars are 2 cm wide: STEPS measured heights to the whole centimeter, so each bar holds two whole centimeters, and no bar gets more steps of the ruler than another.
  • Click: women and men. The same bars, colored by sex, women at the bottom, so the outline doesn’t change. Most of the shorter adults are women, most of the taller ones men, and the middle is where they overlap. Women are about two thirds of the bars: STEPS picks one person per household, and many young men were working abroad (Nepal’s ages, at the end of this part, show who’s missing). The survey’s weights count each man for more, bringing men to 47%; we use the people as measured, since weighting doesn’t change any of the shapes or conclusions here.
  • Click: the bars separate, women’s half of each bar on the left and men’s on the right. Now label each crowd. Center: the mean, women about 151 cm, men about 161. Spread: the standard deviation, about 6.5 cm for women and 7.5 for men: a typical woman is within about 6.5 cm of the average woman. These two numbers label a shape, they don’t replace it. The curves over the bars are Normals with each crowd’s mean and SD; the next slide names them. Each fits its crowd well, better than one Normal fits everyone, because everyone together leans to the right.
  • Click: they stack back up, and the dashed line is the two Normals added together: one lopsided bell, following the bars. So why only one hump, when men are 10 cm taller? Two equal-sized bells only make two humps if they’re more than about 2 SDs apart, and these are about 1.4. With women two thirds of the adults, it would take even more: men would have to be about 9 cm taller again before a dip appeared.
  • The lesson: two groups in a population don’t show up as two humps unless they’re far apart. Don’t read groups off a histogram, in either direction: a single hump can hide two crowds, and a lump or dip in a small sample can be chance. Before explaining a bump or a dip, ask whether it’s bigger than chance, and whether it came from how the data were measured. Nepal’s ages, at the end of this part, come back to that.
  • Twelve impossible measurements are left out (a 261 cm man; heights typed into the weight column). Real survey data needs that kind of checking.

The Normal distribution

  • The same two crowds, side by side. Each curve is a Normal distribution with that crowd’s mean and SD, and the bars follow it closely: two numbers describe the whole shape. That’s the Normal’s main attraction.
  • The Normal is a shape that turns up everywhere, in math and in nature. Don’t explain why yet: what makes it special comes later (Part 2’s Galton board shows where it comes from). For now it’s enough that the heights in a population often take this shape.
  • Click: the bands. Every Normal has the same landmarks, whatever its center and spread: about 68% of cases within 1 SD, 95% within 2 SD. The text checks them against each crowd: 71% and 95% for women, 70% and 95% for men. Close. That’s a good habit: check a rule of thumb against your actual data.
  • A caution if anyone asks: everyone together passes the same check (67% and 96%), even though it’s the lopsided one. The landmarks can’t tell a bell from a slightly lopsided bell; look at the shape too.
  • “Within 2 SD” comes back in Part 2: it’s how we’ll say how sure we are.
  • Real data are never perfectly Normal. The Normal is a description, not a law. The rest of this part is shapes that aren’t bells.

Taller or heavier?

  • Pose the puzzle and let them try. The plots show where he sits in each crowd: to the right in both. But which is further? Each histogram has its own ruler, centimeters and kilograms, and there’s no way to say whether 10 cm is more than 16 kg.
  • Click: change the ruler. Watch the two histograms stretch until their averages line up and their spreads match: the ruler now counts standard deviations from the average. That’s a z-score: how far from the average, measured in the crowd’s own spread. The side shows the arithmetic.
  • This is the first thing z-scores buy us: they take away the arbitrary units. Once both are in SDs, his two lines can be read off one ruler: +1.25 for height, +1.52 for weight. He’s more unusually heavy than tall.
  • Click: the second thing they buy us. A z-score places one person in the crowd. The shading is everyone below him: taller than 88% of adults, heavier than 92%. Because these crowds are roughly Normal, the z-score alone tells you about where he falls: +1 is taller than about 84% (5 in 6), +2 taller than about 98%.
  • Invite someone to say their own height and weight (or make one up), and drag the lines there. Try a short, heavy person: their z-scores have opposite signs.

How old is he?

  • Start with the photo. Ask the room: how old is he? Collect a few guesses. Nobody says one number with certainty. They say “about 40, maybe older.” That sentence is a distribution.
  • Walk through the four shapes, one click each. Uninformed: flat, every age equally plausible. Weakly informed: a wide hump around 40. Strongly informed: a narrow hump. Domain-specific: a plateau between 30 and 50, dropping off outside.
  • The point: a certainty distribution is a picture of “how plausible is each value?” People reason this way all the time. We’re drawing it, exactly as students drew the mayor’s prior last week.
  • Contrast with Part 1, and say it slowly: there’s only one man and one true age. Nothing varies here except how sure we are. The curves are in green for that reason.
  • Bridge: you’ve all guessed. What if we pooled the guesses? Next slide.

One man, one true age. The only thing that varies is how sure we are.

Uninformed I have no idea. He could be between 0 and 100 and I don’t think any possibility is more likely than any other.

Weakly informed He seems like he’s about 40, but I could be very wrong about that. Anything between 20 and 60 seems plausible and even more extreme values are possible.

Strongly informed I’m quite certain that he’s forty or relatively close to it.

Domain-specific I’m fairly sure he’s between 30 and 50, but I have no idea where in that range he lands. Older or younger than that seems unlikely, though.

Ask the room

  • The bridge from “How old is he?” to the Galton board. Last slide: one person, unsure of one man’s age. This one: many people, each unsure, and what happens when we pool them.
  • The room is simulated: each made-up guesser is off by a random amount, too old or too young. (In the simulation he’s 42; don’t reveal that yet.)
  • Click “Ask a room” (20 people). The blue balls are their guesses, one each. Each guess is one person’s belief, and they scatter widely: 30 here, 55 there. That’s variation, among people.
  • The green line is the room’s average, and it drops into the bottom row as a green ball. Ask another room, and another: each room’s guesses scatter just as widely, but their averages land close together.
  • Click “Ask 50 rooms”, a couple of times. The green balls pile into a narrow bell, far narrower than the single guesses. That pile is a certainty distribution: if all we had was one room’s average, this is how far off it could be. Tick “Show his age”: the pile sits on it.
  • Now 1 person per room, start over, ask 50 rooms twice: the pile is as wide as single guesses, because one guess is all the evidence. Then 5 per room: in between. More evidence, a narrower pile: we’re more sure, though people are just as different as before.
  • The click: one guess is anyone’s guess; the average of a room is close, every time. Variation is in the world (the guessers really differ); uncertainty is in us (how sure we can be about his age), and evidence shrinks it.
  • Why does the pile come out as a bell? Each average adds up many random errors, some too high and some too low, and most of them cancel. The next slide does the same thing with balls and pegs.

Random individuals, predictable crowds

  • The callback: the room’s averages piled into a bell. Why a bell? Here is the same thing with balls: each ball adds up random bounces, the way each average adds up random errors.
  • This is a Galton board, after Francis Galton, who built one in the 1870s. At every peg, a ball bounces left or right with equal chance: a coin flip.
  • Start with 4 rows. Drop one ball. Ask: where will the next one land? Nobody knows. Drop a few more, one at a time.
  • Tick “Count the paths.” The numbers under the bins, 1, 4, 6, 4, 1, are the garden of forking paths from Week 1: four coin flips, and the number of ways to get each number of heads. A ball that bounces right twice can do it 6 ways; all the way right, only 1. The middle bins have more paths, so more balls end up there.
  • Drop 100, then 1,000. The bars fill in the dashed outline: the shape the counting predicts.
  • Now set 12 or 14 rows, empty the bins, and drop 1,000. A bell. Drop another 1,000: the same bell.
  • The line on the slide: one ball is anyone’s guess, a thousand make the same shape every time. Random individuals, predictable crowds. That’s why statistics works at all. We can’t predict one admission, one patient, one voter. We can describe the crowd, sometimes very precisely.
  • Say it once: randomness isn’t the opposite of pattern. Randomness has patterns.
  • The bridge to the next slide: this is where named shapes come from. A process, repeated many times, makes a predictable shape. The Galton board’s process (coin flips, added up) makes a bell, the Normal, like the heights in Part 1. Other processes make other shapes, and the next slide names them.

Every named distribution is a story

  • The goal is to demystify the names. Normal, Poisson, Beta sound like jargon. Each one is just the shape a particular process produces, and the process is something students already understand.
  • Walk the six, briefly, with the process first:
    • Normal: many small pushes added up. The Galton board on the last slide, the heights in Part 1.
    • Log-normal: many small pushes multiplied. A raise is a percentage of what you already earn, so earnings spread out at the top. The made-up curves in the opening puzzle were log-normal.
    • Exponential: waiting until something happens. Most waits are short; a few are long.
    • Binomial: counting yeses out of n tries. Week 1’s poll: of 20 people asked, how many say “mayor”? The Galton board is one too: a count of rightward bounces out of n pegs.
    • Poisson: counting events with no maximum. Births in a day at a health post: usually 2 or 3, sometimes 8.
    • Beta: a share between 0 and 1. Literacy rates across districts.
  • These are population distributions, so they’re blue. The heights use Nepal’s real mean and SD; the other knobs are made up for the example.
  • Click: know the process, and you know the shape. The knobs (parameters) slide and stretch it. That’s all a “parameter” is.

  • Beta: try 1 and 1 (flat), then 3 and 7, then 30 and 70. Same center, narrower: a and b work like yeses and noes, so more of them means more certainty. That’s Part 2’s lesson, in a shape.
  • Log-normal: an SD of the log of 0.5, then 1. The tail stretches out to the right.
  • Don’t drill the formulas. The point: a few knobs control a shape, and the situation decides which shape.

server <- function(input, output, session) {
  draws <- reactiveVal(numeric(0))

  observeEvent(input$axis_modal, {
    showModal(
      modalDialog(
        title = "Axis limits",
        checkboxInput(
          "manual_xlim",
          "Manual x-axis range",
          value = isTRUE(input$manual_xlim)
        ),
        conditionalPanel(
          condition = "input.manual_xlim === true",
          numericInput("xlim_min", "x-min", value = input$xlim_min %||% NA),
          numericInput("xlim_max", "x-max", value = input$xlim_max %||% NA)
        ),
        checkboxInput(
          "manual_ylim",
          "Manual y-axis range",
          value = isTRUE(input$manual_ylim)
        ),
        conditionalPanel(
          condition = "input.manual_ylim === true",
          numericInput("ylim_min", "y-min", value = input$ylim_min %||% NA),
          numericInput("ylim_max", "y-max", value = input$ylim_max %||% NA)
        ),
        footer = tagList(
          tags$button(
            type = "button",
            class = "btn btn-secondary",
            `data-bs-dismiss` = "modal",
            style = "padding-left:10px; padding-right:10px;",
            "Close"
          )
        )
      )
    )
  })

  observeEvent(
    list(
      input$trialCount,
      input$successRate,
      input$bern_p,
      input$distType,
      input$mean,
      input$sd,
      input$lnorm_meanlog,
      input$lnorm_sdlog,
      input$umin,
      input$umax,
      input$exp_rate,
      input$poisson_lambda,
      input$beta_alpha,
      input$beta_beta,
      input$gamma_shape,
      input$gamma_rate,
      input$manual_xlim,
      input$xlim_min,
      input$xlim_max,
      input$manual_ylim,
      input$ylim_min,
      input$ylim_max
    ),
    {
      draws(numeric(0))
    },
    ignoreInit = TRUE
  )

  # helper: sanitize a positive numeric input
  safe_pos <- function(x, fallback = 1) {
    if (is.null(x) || !is.finite(x) || x <= 0) fallback else x
  }
  safe_nonneg <- function(x, fallback = 1) {
    if (is.null(x) || !is.finite(x) || x < 0) fallback else x
  }

  add_draws <- function(k) {
    new <- switch(
      input$distType,
      "Bernoulli" = rbinom(k, size = 1, prob = input$bern_p / 100),
      "Binomial" = rbinom(
        k,
        size = input$trialCount,
        prob = input$successRate / 100
      ),
      "Normal" = rnorm(k, mean = input$mean, sd = safe_pos(input$sd)),
      "Log-normal" = rlnorm(
        k,
        meanlog = input$lnorm_meanlog,
        sdlog = safe_pos(input$lnorm_sdlog)
      ),
      "Uniform" = runif(k, min = input$umin, max = input$umax),
      "Exponential" = rexp(k, rate = safe_pos(input$exp_rate)),
      "Poisson" = rpois(k, lambda = safe_nonneg(input$poisson_lambda)),
      "Beta" = rbeta(
        k,
        shape1 = safe_pos(input$beta_alpha),
        shape2 = safe_pos(input$beta_beta)
      ),
      "Gamma" = rgamma(
        k,
        shape = safe_pos(input$gamma_shape),
        rate = safe_pos(input$gamma_rate)
      ),
      numeric(0)
    )
    draws(c(draws(), new))
  }

  observeEvent(input$draw1, add_draws(1))
  observeEvent(input$draw10, add_draws(10))
  observeEvent(input$draw100, add_draws(100))
  observeEvent(input$draw1000, add_draws(1000))
  observeEvent(input$reset, {
    # clear simulated draws
    draws(numeric(0))

    # reset UI controls to their defaults (match UI definitions)
    updateSelectizeInput(session, "distType", selected = "Binomial")

    updateSliderInput(session, "bern_p", value = 50)

    updateSliderInput(session, "successRate", value = 50)
    updateNumericInput(session, "trialCount", value = 10)

    updateNumericInput(session, "mean", value = 0)
    updateNumericInput(session, "sd", value = 1)

    updateNumericInput(session, "lnorm_meanlog", value = 0)
    updateNumericInput(session, "lnorm_sdlog", value = 0.5)

    updateNumericInput(session, "umin", value = 0)
    updateNumericInput(session, "umax", value = 1)

    updateNumericInput(session, "exp_rate", value = 1)
    updateNumericInput(session, "poisson_lambda", value = 1)

    updateNumericInput(session, "beta_alpha", value = 2)
    updateNumericInput(session, "beta_beta", value = 2)

    updateNumericInput(session, "gamma_shape", value = 2)
    updateNumericInput(session, "gamma_rate", value = 1)

    updateCheckboxInput(session, "manual_xlim", value = FALSE)
    updateNumericInput(session, "xlim_min", value = NA)
    updateNumericInput(session, "xlim_max", value = NA)

    updateCheckboxInput(session, "manual_ylim", value = FALSE)
    updateNumericInput(session, "ylim_min", value = NA)
    updateNumericInput(session, "ylim_max", value = NA)
  })

  output$plot <- renderGirafe({
    sim <- draws()

    # ---------- helpers ----------
    empty_plot <- function() girafe(ggplot(), width_svg = 12, height_svg = 8)

    # common theme
    base_theme <- theme_minimal() +
      theme(plot.margin = margin(10, 30, 30, 10))

    # ---------- spec builder ----------
    get_dist_spec <- function(sim) {
      dist <- input$distType

      # ---- BERNOULLI ----
      if (dist == "Bernoulli") {
        p <- input$bern_p / 100
        theory <- data.frame(
          x = 0:1,
          prob = dbinom(0:1, 1, p)
        )

        list(
          type = "discrete",
          theory = theory,
          xlim = c(0, 1),
          ymax = max(theory$prob),
          xlabel = "Outcome (0/1)",
          ylabel = "Probability",
          tooltip_theory = function(df) {
            paste0(
              "Outcome = ",
              df$x,
              "<br>Probability = ",
              round(df$prob, 4)
            )
          }
        )
      } else if (dist == "Binomial") {
        # ---- BINOMIAL ----
        n <- input$trialCount
        p <- input$successRate / 100

        theory <- data.frame(
          x = 0:n,
          prob = dbinom(0:n, n, p)
        )

        list(
          type = "discrete",
          theory = theory,
          xlim = c(0, n),
          ymax = max(theory$prob),
          xlabel = "Number of successes",
          ylabel = "Probability",
          tooltip_theory = function(df) {
            paste0(
              "Success = ",
              df$x,
              " out of ",
              n,
              "<br>Probability = ",
              round(df$prob, 4)
            )
          }
        )
      } else if (dist == "Normal") {
        # ---- NORMAL ----
        mu <- input$mean
        sd <- safe_pos(input$sd)

        x_min <- mu - 4 * sd
        x_max <- mu + 4 * sd

        xs <- seq(x_min, x_max, length.out = 400)
        theory <- data.frame(
          x = xs,
          density = dnorm(xs, mu, sd)
        )

        list(
          dist = "Normal",
          params = list(mu = mu, sd = sd),
          type = "continuous",
          theory = theory,
          xlim = c(x_min, x_max),
          ymax = max(theory$density),
          xlabel = "Value",
          ylabel = "Density"
        )
      } else if (dist == "Log-normal") {
        # ---- LOG-NORMAL ----
        meanlog <- input$lnorm_meanlog
        sdlog <- safe_pos(input$lnorm_sdlog)

        x_min <- 0
        x_max <- qlnorm(.995, meanlog, sdlog)

        xs <- seq(x_min, x_max, length.out = 400)
        theory <- data.frame(
          x = xs,
          density = dlnorm(xs, meanlog, sdlog)
        )

        list(
          type = "continuous",
          theory = theory,
          xlim = c(x_min, x_max),
          ymax = max(theory$density),
          xlabel = "Value",
          ylabel = "Density"
        )
      } else if (dist == "Uniform") {
        # ---- UNIFORM ----
        a <- input$umin
        b <- input$umax
        if (b <= a) {
          return(NULL)
        }

        xs <- seq(a, b, length.out = 400)
        theory <- data.frame(
          x = xs,
          density = dunif(xs, a, b)
        )

        list(
          type = "continuous",
          theory = theory,
          xlim = c(a, b),
          ymax = max(theory$density),
          xlabel = "Value",
          ylabel = "Density",
          expand_mult = c(0, .85)
        )
      } else if (dist == "Exponential") {
        # ---- EXPONENTIAL ----
        rate <- safe_pos(input$exp_rate)

        x_min <- 0
        x_max <- qexp(.999, rate)

        xs <- seq(x_min, x_max, length.out = 400)
        theory <- data.frame(
          x = xs,
          density = dexp(xs, rate)
        )

        list(
          type = "continuous",
          theory = theory,
          xlim = c(x_min, x_max),
          ymax = max(theory$density),
          xlabel = "Value",
          ylabel = "Density"
        )
      } else if (dist == "Poisson") {
        # ---- POISSON ----
        lambda <- safe_nonneg(input$poisson_lambda)

        x_max_theory <- ceiling(qpois(.999, lambda))
        x_max_sim <- if (length(sim)) {
          max(ceiling(sim), x_max_theory)
        } else {
          x_max_theory
        }
        xs <- 0:x_max_sim

        theory <- data.frame(
          x = xs,
          prob = dpois(xs, lambda)
        )

        list(
          type = "discrete",
          theory = theory,
          xlim = c(0, x_max_sim),
          ymax = max(theory$prob),
          xlabel = "k",
          ylabel = "Probability",
          tooltip_theory = function(df) {
            paste0("k = ", df$x, "<br>Probability = ", round(df$prob, 4))
          }
        )
      } else if (dist == "Beta") {
        # ---- BETA ----
        a <- safe_pos(input$beta_alpha)
        b <- safe_pos(input$beta_beta)

        xs <- seq(0, 1, length.out = 400)
        theory <- data.frame(
          x = xs,
          density = dbeta(xs, a, b)
        )

        list(
          type = "continuous",
          theory = theory,
          xlim = c(0, 1),
          ymax = max(theory$density),
          xlabel = "Value",
          ylabel = "Density"
        )
      } else if (dist == "Gamma") {
        # ---- GAMMA ----
        shape <- safe_pos(input$gamma_shape)
        rate <- safe_pos(input$gamma_rate)

        x_min <- 0
        x_max <- qgamma(.999, shape, rate)

        xs <- seq(x_min, x_max, length.out = 400)
        theory <- data.frame(
          x = xs,
          density = dgamma(xs, shape, rate)
        )

        list(
          type = "continuous",
          theory = theory,
          xlim = c(x_min, x_max),
          ymax = max(theory$density),
          xlabel = "Value",
          ylabel = "Density"
        )
      } else {
        NULL
      }
    }

    # ---------- build plot ----------
    spec <- get_dist_spec(sim)
    if (is.null(spec)) {
      return(empty_plot())
    }

    apply_manual_xlim <- function(spec) {
      if (!isTRUE(input$manual_xlim)) {
        return(spec)
      }

      xmin <- input$xlim_min
      xmax <- input$xlim_max

      if (
        is.null(xmin) ||
          is.null(xmax) ||
          !is.finite(xmin) ||
          !is.finite(xmax) ||
          xmin >= xmax
      ) {
        return(spec)
      }

      spec$xlim <- c(xmin, xmax)

      if (spec$type == "continuous") {
        if (identical(spec$dist, "Normal")) {
          mu <- spec$params$mu
          sd <- spec$params$sd
          xs <- seq(xmin, xmax, length.out = 400)
          spec$theory <- data.frame(
            x = xs,
            density = dnorm(xs, mu, sd)
          )
        } else {
          spec$theory <- spec$theory[
            spec$theory$x >= xmin & spec$theory$x <= xmax,
            ,
            drop = FALSE
          ]
        }
        ymax <- max(spec$theory$density, na.rm = TRUE)
        if (is.finite(ymax)) {
          spec$ymax <- ymax
        }
      } else {
        spec$theory <- spec$theory[
          spec$theory$x >= xmin & spec$theory$x <= xmax,
          ,
          drop = FALSE
        ]
        ymax <- max(spec$theory$prob, na.rm = TRUE)
        if (is.finite(ymax)) {
          spec$ymax <- ymax
        }
      }

      spec
    }

    spec <- apply_manual_xlim(spec)

    apply_manual_ylim <- function(spec) {
      if (!isTRUE(input$manual_ylim)) {
        return(spec)
      }

      ymin <- input$ylim_min
      ymax <- input$ylim_max

The same shapes, for certainty

  • The same families, now in green: they describe how sure we are, not how cases vary.

Correlation is average agreement

  • Mechanics slide. The formula says: multiply each person’s two z-scores, then average.
  • The plain-language version is what matters: each person votes. Same side of average on both is a positive vote. Opposite sides is a negative vote. r is the average vote.
  • Properties worth remembering: always between −1 and +1, no units, and it doesn’t care whether you measure in cm or inches.

\[ r = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^{n} z_{\text{height},i} \times z_{\text{weight},i} \]

  • Each person “votes”:
    • Same side of average on both → positive vote
    • Opposite sides → negative vote
  • r is the average vote.

Why we always standardize

  • Mechanics slide, and a rule for the rest of the course: every regression is fit in z-scores. Three reasons.
  • No intercept: both variables have mean 0, so the line goes through the middle. One fewer number to estimate, and to set a prior for.
  • Coefficients compare: every β is in SDs per SD, so living area and bedrooms, or height and age, sit on one scale. The houses’ model builder relies on this.
  • One prior fits every β: Normal(0, 1) says we’re 95% sure a 1 SD change in the predictor moves the outcome by less than 2 SD. That’s generous: with one predictor, β is the correlation, which can’t pass 1.
  • The table: the same relationship in four units. In kg per cm, kg per inch and lb per inch the number changes, so a sensible prior in one unit is wrong in the next. In SDs it’s the same number, whatever the units.
  • At the end, convert back to real units, as on “Reading the coefficient.” The R code is one line per variable; the homework uses it.

Step 5: One coefficient

  • Mechanics slide. Same as the height and weight coefficient, now for price and living area.
  • In z-scores: one SD more space goes with about 0.71 SD higher price. In dollars per m², convert with the SDs.
  • Again: “goes with,” not “adds.”

\[ z_{\text{price}} = \beta \times z_{\text{area}} \]

  • In z-scores: β = 0.71
    • “A house 1 SD larger than average sells, on average, for 0.71 SD more.”
  • In real units: 0.71 × $79,887 / 47.0 m² ≈ $1,202 per m²
    • “Each extra square meter of living space goes with about $1,202 more in price.”

The model, written formally

  • Mechanics slide. The same notation as the mayor’s model last week: “∼” reads “is drawn from.”
  • Line 1: prices spread around a center. Line 2: the center moves with the features.
  • Walk the table against the model builder: μ is the prediction, the βs are the bars, σ is the typical miss.
  • The last bullets tie the week together: every line of a model is a distribution, and the three kinds all appear. “The others held fixed” is the part to underline. It’s why every coefficient depends on what else is in the model.

\[ \begin{aligned} z_{\text{price},i} &\sim \text{Normal}(\mu_i,\ \sigma) && \class{blue}{\text{how prices vary around the prediction}} \\ \mu_i &= \beta_1 z_{\text{bedrooms},i} + \beta_2 z_{\text{area},i} + \dots && \class{orange}{\text{how the center moves with each feature}} \end{aligned} \]

Symbol Read it as In the model builder
\(\mu_i\) the center of the price distribution for houses like house i the prediction
\(\beta_1, \beta_2, \dots\) how far the center moves per SD of each feature, the others held fixed the bars
\(\sigma\) how far prices spread around that center the typical miss
  • Each line is a distribution: prices spread around a center (population), the center moves with the features (correlative), and every β is a number we’re unsure about (certainty, the intervals).
