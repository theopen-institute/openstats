Data Lunch

Statistics from the ground up · The Open Institute For Social Science

Data Lunch is a short course in applied statistics. It starts from a single uncomfortable claim, that most published research findings are false, and rebuilds statistics so you can tell when a finding deserves to be believed.

The course is Bayesian, in the style of McElreath’s Statistical Rethinking. We think about causes before we touch the data, draw our assumptions as causal diagrams (DAGs), write models in Stan, and check every model on simulated data before trusting it on real data. Students apply each week’s ideas to their own question using World Bank development indicators.

Lectures

Each week is a slide deck. Most weeks have a concepts part and an applied part.

Week Topic What you’ll learn
Week 1 A fresh start Why most published research is false: statistical significance, the base rate paradox, and Bayesian updating.
Week 2 Describing data Probability distributions, z-scores, correlation, and our first regression coefficient. Applied: what makes a house expensive?
Week 3 Causes and DAGs Drawing what causes what, what “cause” means in statistics, and the workflow from estimand to estimate. Worked example: does sex affect weight?
Week 4 Confounding The three building blocks of every DAG (fork, pipe, collider), the paradoxes each one creates, and how to decide what to control for.
Week 5 Acting on evidence Seven questions to ask of any finding, turning a decision into an estimand, and designing the experiment you wish you could run.

A Bayesian workflow

A Bayesian Workflow is a full worked example that pulls the course together. It asks whether a university’s admissions process discriminates by sex, and goes through ten steps in three parts:

Scientific model

  1. Ask the question.
  2. Draw the causal model (DAG).
  3. Define the estimand.
  4. Simulate the population.

Statistical model

  1. Write the formal model.
  2. Write the Stan code.
  3. Test on simulated data.

Analysis

  1. Fit and validate the model.
  2. Compute the estimate.
  3. Test its robustness.

Along the way it shows Simpson’s paradox: a large overall gap in admission rates that disappears once we compare applicants to the same department.

  • Week 2 homework: distributions, z-scores, correlation, and regression.
  • Week 3 homework: DAGs, causes, and the workflow.
  • Week 4 homework: forks, pipes, colliders, and what to control for.
  • Week 5 homework: evaluating findings, estimands, and target trials.
