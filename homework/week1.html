Week 1 Problem Types

Probability, base rates, and Bayesian updating: problem types and difficulty levels

This page defines the kinds of problems that go with Week 1, rather than a fixed list of questions. Each problem type is one skill from the lecture, written so that a generator could produce new problems of that type: its parameters, the template the problem follows, how to compute the answer, and the mistakes students usually make.

Every type comes in three levels:

Level What it asks for
1. Direct One step. Round numbers, everything needed is given, and the question names the quantity to find.
2. Multi-step Two or more steps, or an intermediate quantity the student has to work out first. Realistic, less round numbers. Often a comparison between two cases.
3. Transfer The same idea used in an unfamiliar way: working backwards from an answer, chaining two steps of reasoning, or explaining why in words.

Conventions for every type

  • Answers are given as a fraction or count ratio, a decimal (2–3 places) and a percentage. Accept any of the three, rounded sensibly.
  • Counts must be whole numbers. When a problem builds a table of counts for a population, choose the population size so that every cell is a whole number (or say “about” when it can’t be, as with 49.5 → “about 50”).
  • Contexts should be familiar to students in Nepal where possible: Kathmandu weather, local elections, schools and universities, clinics, cricket, buses. Each type lists a few.
  • Common mistakes under each type are the errors students actually make; a multiple-choice version can use them as wrong options.

T1. Counting outcomes

Skill. Probability is counting: count everything that could happen, count the ways we care about, and compare. (Lecture: “Probability is just counting”, “Counting the ways a thing could happen”.)

Parameters. n outcomes or trials, k outcomes of interest, the kind of event (single draw, repeated coin flips, dice).

Level The problem Example Answer
1 One draw from equally likely outcomes: k of n. A raffle sells 400 tickets and you buy 8. What is the probability you win? 8 / 400 = 0.02 = 2%
2 Repeated events, small enough to list every path: exactly k successes in n = 2–4 trials. Flip a coin 3 times. What is the probability of exactly 2 heads? 3 of 8 paths = 0.375
3 Too many paths to list (n = 5–8), or “at least” / “at most” events. Flip a coin 6 times. What is the probability of at least 5 heads? (6 + 1) / 64 = 7/64 ≈ 0.109

Answer. k / n for a single draw. For repeated fair coin flips, the number of paths with exactly k heads is C(n, k), out of 2n.

Common mistakes. Counting outcomes that aren’t equally likely as if they were (two dice: “11 possible sums, so P(7) = 1/11”); forgetting that HHT, HTH and THH are different paths; adding paths for “at least” but leaving out the top case.

Contexts. Raffles, dice, coin flips, picking a student at random from a class (“15 of 25 came by bus”).

T2. Writing probabilities

Skill. A probability is a number from 0 to 1 or a percentage; the two convert by moving the decimal point two places. Probabilities can be ordered from impossible to certain. (Lecture: “Writing it down: 0 to 1”.)

Parameters. A count ratio or a probability in one form; for Level 2, a set of 3–5 events to compare; for Level 3, very small probabilities or complements.

Level The problem Example Answer
1 Convert between a count ratio, a decimal and a percentage. 3 of 20 students walk to school. Write this as a decimal and a percentage. 0.15, 15%
2 Compute several probabilities from counts and put them in order. Order from least to most likely: winning a raffle (10 of 500 tickets), rolling a six, rain in Kathmandu (113 of 365 days), a coin landing heads. 0.02 < 0.17 < 0.31 < 0.5
3 Very small or very large probabilities, and complements. Lightning kills about 1 in 7,000,000 people a year. Write that as a decimal and a percentage. What is the probability of no rain on a random day, if it rains on 113 of 365? 0.00000014 = 0.000014%; 1 − 0.31 = 0.69

Answer. Divide the counts; ×100 for a percentage. Complement: 1 − p.

Common mistakes. Moving the decimal point one place, or the wrong way (0.15 → 1.5%); treating “1 in 7,000,000” as 7,000,000 or as 1/7; writing a probability above 1.

Contexts. Kathmandu rain, raffles, dice, exam pass rates, rare risks (lightning, lottery).

T3. Probability notation

Skill. Write a statement as P( event ) or P( event | condition ), and see that P( A | B ) and P( B | A ) describe different groups. (Lecture: “Writing it down: P(event)”, “Writing it down: P(event | condition)”, “Direction of conditions”.)

Parameters. A sentence about a share of some group; for Level 3, a pair of sentences about the same two things in opposite directions.

Level The problem Example Answer
1 No condition: translate a plain share. “40% of students at this university are from Kathmandu.” P( from Kathmandu ) = 0.40
2 With a condition: identify which part is the condition. “Of people who smoke, 15% develop lung cancer.” P( lung cancer | smoker ) = 0.15
3 A reversed pair: translate both and explain how both can be true. “Of smokers, 15% develop lung cancer. Of people with lung cancer, 85% smoked.” P( cancer | smoker ) = 0.15 and P( smoker | cancer ) = 0.85. Different groups: most smokers never get cancer, but cancer is rare and concentrated among smokers.

Answer. The group the sentence is about (“of people who…”, “when…”, “given…”) goes after the bar.

Common mistakes. Putting the condition on the wrong side of the bar; dropping the condition; assuming the two directions must be equal.

Contexts. Health (smoking, symptoms), sport (“when Nepal leads at halftime, they win 70% of the time”), weather by month, education.

T4. Conditional probability from a table

Skill. Read a conditional probability from a table of counts by choosing the right denominator, and compute both directions. (Lecture: “Writing it down: P(event | condition)” (rain by month), “Direction of conditions” (the urban/rural table).)

Parameters. A 2×2 (or 2×m) table of counts; which margins are shown; for Level 3, partial information to rebuild the table from.

Level The problem Example Answer
1 A 2×2 table with totals given: read one conditional. Urban: 240 sociology, 300 economics (540). Rural: 80 sociology, 20 economics (100). What is P( Economics | Urban )? 300 / 540 ≈ 0.556
2 No totals given: compute them, then both directions, and explain the difference. Same table without totals. Find P( Urban | Economics ) and P( Economics | Urban ). 300 / 320 ≈ 0.94 and 300 / 540 ≈ 0.56. Different denominators: economics students vs. urban students.
3 Rebuild the table from shares, then answer a conditional; or a table with many columns where the two directions are very different. 640 students; half study sociology; 25% of sociology students are rural; 100 students are rural in all. What is P( Sociology | Rural )? Sociology = 320, rural sociology = 80, so 80 / 100 = 0.80

Answer. P( A | B ) = count( A and B ) / count( B ).

Common mistakes. Dividing by the grand total instead of the row or column total; dividing by the wrong margin (computing P( B | A ) when asked for P( A | B )).

Contexts. Students by field and origin, rainy days by month (P( July | rain ) = 31/179 ≈ 0.17, though P( rain | July ) = 1 in 2025), voters by ward and party, patients by symptom and diagnosis.

T5. Diagnostic tests and the base rate

Skill. When a condition is rare, most positive results can be false even from an accurate test. Build a table of counts from prevalence, sensitivity and specificity, and find the chance that a positive result is real. (Lecture: “Probability for Decision Making”, “What a positive test means”, “The base rate paradox” and its app, “Does this mean tests are useless?”)

Parameters. Prevalence (base rate), sensitivity = P( positive | has it ), specificity = P( negative | doesn’t have it ), population size N.

Level The problem Example Answer
1 Round numbers (prevalence 5–20%, accuracy 80–95%), N given. 1,000 people; 10% have the disease; the test catches 90% of cases and correctly clears 90% of healthy people. What share of positives have the disease? 90 true positives, 90 false positives: 90 / 180 = 50%
2 A rare condition (0.1–1%) and a very accurate test; or compare two settings. HIV prevalence 0.1%, sensitivity 99%, specificity 97%, 100,000 people. Find P( HIV+ | test+ ). Repeat in a clinic where 5% have HIV. 99 / (99 + 2,997) ≈ 3.2%; in the clinic 4,950 / (4,950 + 2,850) ≈ 63.5%
3 Work backwards (what accuracy or prevalence is needed?), or chain two tests. At 0.1% prevalence and 99% sensitivity, what specificity does the test need for at least half of positives to be real? False positives ≤ true positives: 99,900 × (1 − spec) ≤ 99, so specificity ≥ 99.9%

Answer. P( has it | positive ) = prev × sens / ( prev × sens + (1 − prev) × (1 − spec) ). In counts: true positives / (true positives + false positives).

Common mistakes. Answering with the sensitivity (99%) instead of P( has it | positive ); ignoring the base rate; computing false positives from the whole population instead of the healthy group; forgetting that a second test’s “prior” is the first test’s result.

Contexts. HIV and other screening tests, AI-writing detectors, spam filters, airport security, fraud alerts.

T6. How far can we trust a discovery?

Skill. A study is a test of an idea: its sensitivity is the chance it finds a true idea, and its specificity is the chance it correctly finds nothing when the idea is false. When true ideas are rare, most “discoveries” are false. (Lecture: “So why is most published research wrong?”, “Types of research, by the numbers”.)

Parameters. Number of ideas tested, share that are true (base rate), sensitivity (1 − β, also called power), specificity (1 − α).

Level The problem Example Answer
1 Round numbers: count true and false discoveries. 1,000 ideas, 10% true; studies find 80% of true ideas and wrongly “find” 5% of false ones. What share of discoveries are true? 80 true, 45 false: 80 / 125 = 64%
2 Compare study types: change the base rate, sensitivity or specificity, and say which matters most. Compare 1% vs 10% true ideas and 20% vs 80% sensitivity (specificity 95%). 1%: 3.9% and 13.9%. 10%: 30.8% and 64.0%. The base rate matters most.
3 Work backwards, or model p-hacking as lower specificity. With 80% sensitivity and 95% specificity, what share of ideas must be true for half of discoveries to be true? 0.8 b = 0.05 (1 − b), so b ≈ 5.9%

Answer. Same formula as T5, with the base rate as prevalence. α = 1 − specificity, β = 1 − sensitivity.

Common mistakes. Treating α = 5% as “5% of discoveries are false”; thinking higher sensitivity fixes a low base rate; forgetting that p-hacking raises the false-positive rate (lowers specificity).

Contexts. Drug trials, education programs, psychology experiments, “a new study shows…” headlines.

T7. Which direction does the conditional run?

Skill. Tell P( data | hypothesis ) apart from P( hypothesis | data ), and recognize when a claim reads one as the other. (Lecture: “Statistical significance”, “Statistical significance (revisited)”, “Direction of conditions”.)

Parameters. A statement or claim (a p-value interpretation, an accuracy claim, a headline); which direction it states and which it implies.

Level The problem Example Answer
1 Classify a statement as P( data | hypothesis ) or P( hypothesis | data ). “If the treatment did nothing, results this strong would happen 3% of the time.” P( data | hypothesis ): the chance of the data, assuming no effect
2 Spot a reversed reading and say what it should be. A classmate gets p = 0.03 and says, “So there’s only a 3% chance my hypothesis is wrong.” The p-value is P( data this extreme | no effect ), not P( no effect | data ).
3 Write a short, fair correction of a realistic claim, saying what extra information would be needed. “The detector is 95% accurate, so if it flags your essay, you cheated.” 95% is P( flag | AI ). The question is P( AI | flag ), which also depends on how many essays are AI-written (a base rate).

Answer key (rubric). Names both conditionals correctly; says which one the claim needs; identifies the missing ingredient (usually a base rate or prior).

Common mistakes. Treating the two directions as interchangeable; arguing that the test or method is “wrong” rather than that the claim asks a different question.

Contexts. p-value interpretations, test accuracy claims, news headlines, policy reports.

T8. Prior, likelihood and posterior in a story

Skill. Find the three parts of Bayesian updating in an everyday story: what we believed before, what we observed, and what we believe now. (Lecture: “Bayesian Updating: What it is”, “You already understand Bayesian updating”.)

Parameters. A story template with a starting belief, an observation and (for Levels 1–2) an updated belief; for Level 3, a second observer or a change in the evidence.

Level The problem Example Answer
1 All three parts are stated: label them. A doctor thinks a cough is probably a cold. An X-ray shows a shadow on the lung, and now the doctor thinks pneumonia is more likely. Prior: probably a cold. Data: the X-ray. Posterior: pneumonia more likely.
2 The posterior isn’t stated: say which way the belief should move, and why. You expect the bus to Patan to take about 30 minutes. Three days in a row it takes 50 minutes. Toward a longer trip time, because the observations fit a longer trip better.
3 Explain the size of the update, or compare two people with different priors. “I thought 60% would vote for the mayor; a survey said 20%; now I think about 45%.” Why 45% and not 20%? What would move it closer to 20%? The posterior combines prior and data. A bigger survey, or a weaker prior, would move it closer to 20%.

Answer key (rubric). Each part identified correctly; the direction of the update matches the evidence; Level 3 names the strength of the prior and the amount of data.

Common mistakes. Calling the observation the posterior; expecting the posterior to equal the data; ignoring the prior entirely.

Contexts. Medicine, travel times, elections, weather, cricket form.

T9. Bayesian updating by counting

Skill. Update a belief over several possibilities by counting the ways each one could produce the data: prior × ways, then divide by the total. (Lecture: the bag of stones app, “Bayes’ theorem”.)

Parameters. Number of stones in the bag M (3–9), which bags are possible (two, or all M + 1), the prior over bags (equal, or given), and a sequence of draws with replacement.

Level The problem Example Answer
1 Two possible bags, equal prior, one or two draws. A bag of 4 stones has either 1 or 3 blue stones, equally likely. You draw one stone: blue. How likely is the 3-blue bag? Ways: 1 vs 3, so 3 / 4 = 0.75
2 Every possible bag, equal prior, 2–3 draws: fill in a table of ways and posteriors. A bag of 3 stones holds 0–3 blue stones, each equally likely. Draws: blue, black, blue. Ways k × (3 − k) × k: 0, 2, 4, 0. Posterior: 1 blue = 1/3, 2 blue = 2/3.
3 Sequential updating (yesterday’s posterior is today’s prior), order of draws, or an unequal prior. 9 stones, equal prior, draws blue, black, blue (posterior peaks at 6 blue, 0.20). Draw one more blue. Update, then check against updating on all four draws at once. Ways k³ (9 − k), total 2,892. Peak moves to 7 blue (0.237). The same either way.

Answer. For k blue stones out of M, each blue draw contributes k, each black draw M − k. Posterior ∝ prior × product of ways; divide by the sum over bags.

Common mistakes. Forgetting to divide by the total; thinking the order of draws matters (with replacement it doesn’t); not ruling out impossible bags (0 blue after a blue draw); assuming the most plausible bag is certain.

Contexts. The bag of stones; a box of tickets; a basket of good and bad mangoes; share of supporters among a few households.

T10. Observation and interpretation

Skill. Separate the observed part of a claim (what was counted, measured or recorded) from the interpretation built on it (the assumptions), in both statistical and ethnographic work. (Lecture: “Most studies are wrong…”, “Numbers don’t speak for themselves”, “The science comes first”.)

Parameters. A claim (statistical, ethnographic, or one of each); for Levels 2–3, an alternative assumption that would change the conclusion.

Level The problem Example Answer
1 Name the observation and one assumption behind a conclusion. “It rained on 179 of 365 days in 2025, so there’s a 49% chance of rain tomorrow.” Observation: 179 rainy days. Assumption: tomorrow is like an average day in 2025 (ignores the season).
2 Show how a different assumption changes the conclusion. “Only 30% of 20 people surveyed back the mayor, so the mayor will lose.” Observation: 6 of 20. Assumptions: the 20 represent voters, people answered honestly, they will vote. A small or unrepresentative sample could change the conclusion.
3 Compare a statistical and an ethnographic claim, and propose an observation that would test an assumption. A survey finds that 70% of households trust the ward office; an ethnographer concludes from interviews that trust is low. Each joins observations with assumptions (who answered, what “trust” means, whose voices stand for others). Proposals: a follow-up that asks the survey question in interviews, or surveys the interviewed households.

Answer key (rubric). The observation is stated without interpretation; at least one assumption is named; the student explains how the conclusion depends on it; Level 3 treats both kinds of claim even-handedly.

Common mistakes. Treating numbers as automatically objective and interviews as automatically subjective; naming “bias” without saying which assumption.

Contexts. Weather forecasts, polls, program evaluations, ethnographic findings, news reports.
