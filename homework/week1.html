Week 1 Homework

Probability, base rates, and Bayesian updating

These exercises go with the Week 1 lecture. Part A checks your understanding of the ideas. Parts B–D are problems to work through. Part E is an optional R challenge.

A few ground rules:

  • Show your work. A correct number with no reasoning is worth less than a wrong number with clear reasoning. Drawing a table of counts (like we did for the HIV example) is almost always the easiest way to do these problems.
  • Write in plain language. If you can’t explain it to a friend who missed the lecture, you haven’t finished understanding it yet.
  • Problems marked ★ are harder. Give them a real try, but don’t get stuck on them.

Part A: Concepts

A1. In one or two sentences, explain what probability means in this course. Then explain how a Bayesian and a frequentist would each understand the sentence “The shop near my house is probably open right now.”

A2. Write each of these sentences in probability notation, P( event | condition ):

  1. Of people who smoke, 15% develop lung cancer.
  2. Of people with lung cancer, 85% were smokers.
  3. When Nepal leads at halftime, they win 70% of the time.
  4. 40% of students at this university are from Kathmandu.

Statements (a) and (b) are about the same two things, but the numbers are very different. Explain how both can be true at once.

A3. A classmate gets a result with p = 0.03 and says: “So there’s only a 3% chance my hypothesis is wrong.”

  1. Write what a p-value measures in P( … | … ) notation.
  2. Write what your classmate thinks it measures in P( … | … ) notation.
  3. Explain the mistake in your own words.

A4. Match each phrase to its probability statement. (HIV+ means the person has HIV.)

Term Statement
Sensitivity P( test– | HIV+ )
Specificity P( test+ | HIV– )
Type I error (false positive) P( test+ | HIV+ )
Type II error (false negative) P( test– | HIV– )

Then name the probability that a patient who just tested positive actually cares about. Is it one of the four above?

A5. Ioannidis argued that most published research findings are false, and he did it without re-running a single study. In 4–6 sentences, explain how that is possible. Use the words base rate, power, and false positive in your answer.

A6. True or false? Give a one-sentence reason for each.

  1. A test with 99% sensitivity and 99% specificity gives a correct result 99% of the time for someone who tests positive.
  2. If P(A) = P(B), then P(A | B) = P(B | A).
  3. If your prior probability for a hypothesis is exactly 0, no amount of data can change your mind.
  4. If everything else stays the same, lowering α from 0.05 to 0.005 increases the share of significant results that are true.
  5. If most published findings are false, science doesn’t work.

A7. Find the prior, the likelihood (the observation), and the posterior in each story:

  1. A doctor thinks a patient’s cough is probably a cold. A chest X-ray shows a shadow on the lung, and now the doctor thinks pneumonia is more likely.
  2. You expect the bus to Patan to take about 30 minutes. Three days in a row it takes 50 minutes, so you now leave home earlier.
  3. In lecture: “I think about 60% of Kathmandu will vote for the current mayor… a survey says 30%… I now think about 45%.” Why is the posterior between 60% and 30%, and not exactly 30%? What would push it closer to 30%?

Part B: Probability calculations

B1. In 2025, Kathmandu saw rain on 179 of 365 days. In April it rained on 13 of 30 days, and in July on all 31 days.

  1. What is P( no rain )?
  2. What is P( April | rain )? That is, if you pick a rainy day at random from 2025, how likely is it that the day is in April?
  3. Compare P( rain | April ) with P( April | rain ). Why are they so different?
  4. ★ What is P( July | rain )? Why is it nowhere near 1, even though P( rain | July ) = 1?

B2. Use the table of students from the lecture:

Sociology Economics Total
Urban 240 300 540
Rural 80 20 100
Total 320 320 640

Calculate:

  1. P( Urban )
  2. P( Economics | Urban )
  3. P( Urban | Economics )
  4. P( Rural )
  5. ★ Using Bayes’ theorem (not the table), compute P( Sociology | Rural ) from P( Rural | Sociology ) = 0.25, P( Sociology ), and P( Rural ). Check that you get the 0.80 from lecture.

B3. Make your own 2×2 table from something in your life or work: your village, your office, your cricket team, your WhatsApp groups. Use real counts if you have them, or a reasonable guess if you don’t. Then write one pair of conditional probabilities, P(A | B) and P(B | A), that come out very different, and explain why.

Part C: Base rates

For every problem in this part, build a table of counts for a large population (for example 100,000 people), like the one in lecture.

C1. The HIV test, revisited. Sensitivity is 99%, specificity is 97%, and prevalence is 0.1%.

  1. Rebuild the lecture table and confirm that P( HIV+ | test+ ) ≈ 3.2%.
  2. Now the same test is used in a clinic where 5% of patients have HIV. What is P( HIV+ | test+ ) now?
  3. Go back to 0.1% prevalence, but improve the specificity to 99.9%. What is P( HIV+ | test+ )?
  4. In (b) and (c), which change helped more, and why?
  5. What is P( HIV– | test– ) at 0.1% prevalence? Should someone who tests negative worry?

C2. ★ Testing twice. At 0.1% prevalence, a person tests positive. They take a second, independent test (same sensitivity and specificity) and it also comes back positive. What is the probability they have HIV now? Hint: what is the “prior” for the second test?

C3. AI detectors. A university buys a tool that flags essays as written by AI. It correctly flags 95% of AI-written essays, and wrongly flags 2% of essays that students wrote themselves.

  1. If 10% of submitted essays are AI-written, what share of flagged essays are actually AI-written?
  2. What if only 2% of essays are AI-written?
  3. A teacher says, “The detector is 95% accurate, so if it flags you, you cheated.” Write a short reply (3–4 sentences) using what you learned this week.

C4. Ioannidis’s table. In lecture we assumed 1% of tested hypotheses are true, 80% power, and α = 5%. That gave P( true | significant ) ≈ 14%. Using 10,000 tested hypotheses, recompute P( true | significant ) for each case:

Case Base rate Power α
a 1% 50% 0.05
b 10% 80% 0.05
c 1% 80% 0.20 (p-hacking)
d 1% 80% 0.005

Which change makes the biggest difference? What does that suggest a research field could do to publish fewer false findings?

C5. ★ With 80% power and α = 0.05, what base rate would you need for exactly half of significant findings to be true? Set up an equation and solve it.

Part D: Bayesian updating

These problems use the bag of stones from the lecture app. The bag holds 9 stones, each blue (🔵) or black (⚫️). There are 10 possible bags (0 blue, 1 blue, …, 9 blue), and before drawing we think each one is equally likely. We draw one stone at a time, note its color, and put it back.

D1. Before we draw anything:

  1. What is the prior probability of each of the 10 possible bags?
  2. We draw 🔵. Which bag(s) can we now rule out completely? Why?
  3. We draw ⚫️ next. Which bag(s) can we now rule out?

D2. Counting by hand. Our three draws are 🔵 ⚫️ 🔵.

  1. For a bag with k blue stones, the chance of drawing blue is k/9. Write an expression for the likelihood of 🔵 ⚫️ 🔵 for a bag with k blue stones.
  2. Fill in this table. (Tip: the “ways” column is the number of ways to produce the sequence, k × (9 − k) × k. Then divide each row by the total.)
Blue stones (k) Prior Ways to get 🔵⚫️🔵 Posterior
0 0.1
1 0.1
… …
9 0.1
  1. Which bag is now most plausible? How plausible is it? Why aren’t we more certain?

D3. Would the posterior be different if the draws came in the order ⚫️ 🔵 🔵? Explain why or why not.

D4. Today’s posterior is tomorrow’s prior. Start from your posterior in D2 and draw one more stone: 🔵.

  1. Update using your D2 posterior as the new prior. Which bag is most plausible now?
  2. Now start from scratch with an equal prior and update on all four draws at once (🔵 ⚫️ 🔵 🔵). Do you get the same answer? Why does this matter?

D5. A different prior. A friend who packed the bag tells you it holds either 3 blue stones or 6 blue stones, with equal chances. You draw 🔵 🔵 ⚫️. What is the probability the bag has 6 blue stones?

D6. Using the app. Open the Bayesian Updating slide and set the bag to Hidden.

  1. Draw 5 stones. Write down the most plausible bag and its probability.
  2. Keep going to 20 draws, then 50 (hold Shift to draw 10 at a time). Record the same two numbers each time.
  3. Reveal the bag. Were you right? Describe in 2–3 sentences how the shape of the distribution changed as you collected more data.
  4. Reset and do it again with a new hidden bag. Did the second bag ever look like a different bag along the way? What does that tell you about drawing conclusions from a small sample?

Part E: R challenge (optional)

E1. Write an R function p_true_given_sig(base_rate, power, alpha) that returns P( true | significant ). Use it to check your answers to C4. Then make a plot with base rate (0.001 to 0.5) on the x-axis and P( true | significant ) on the y-axis, with one line for 50% power and one for 80% power.

E2. ★ Do D2 and D4 in R. Make a vector k <- 0:9, a prior rep(1/10, 10), and compute the posterior using prior * likelihood / sum(prior * likelihood). Write a loop that updates the posterior one draw at a time for any sequence of draws, such as c("blue", "black", "blue", "blue").

Part F: Reflection

F1. Find a news story that says “a new study shows…” about health, education, or the economy. In a short paragraph:

  • What was the claim?
  • Was this a surprising, novel idea (low base rate) or something many people already expected (higher base rate)?
  • Using what you learned this week, how much should one study change your beliefs?
