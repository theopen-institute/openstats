Week 5 Homework

Reading a finding, asking a question: the seven questions, estimands, and the target trial

These exercises go with the Week 5 lecture. Part A checks your understanding of the ideas. Parts B–E are problems to work through, in the same order as the lecture: the seven questions, the bicycle case, estimands, and the target trial. Part F is the main assignment from the last slide: a critique memo, plus a check on your own project. Part G is an optional R challenge.

A few ground rules:

  • Show your work. A correct answer with no reasoning is worth less than a wrong answer with clear reasoning. For many of these questions, drawing a small DAG is the easiest way to think.
  • Write in plain language. If you can’t explain it to a friend who missed the lecture, you haven’t finished understanding it yet.
  • There is often more than one good answer. This week is about judgement. Say what you assume, and why.
  • Problems marked ★ are harder. Give them a real try, but don’t get stuck on them.

All the studies, programs, and numbers in this homework are made up for practice, unless the question says it comes from the lecture slides.

Part A: Concepts

A1. Write the seven questions to ask of any finding, from memory if you can. Next to each one, write the week of the course it comes from (Week 1, 2, 3 or 4). Which one or two are new this week?

A2. Is each sentence a descriptive, predictive, or causal claim? Underline any causal verbs (words like boosts, drives, leads to, protects against).

  1. “In our survey of 500 households in Dhading, 1 in 4 received money from a family member working abroad.”
  2. “Remittances drive higher school enrollment in the hills.”
  3. “Given the rain so far this monsoon, the river will probably flood in August.”
  4. “Students who use the school library more get better grades.”
  5. “Using the school library boosts grades.”

Pick one causal claim. Rewrite it as an estimand: what number would answer it? Is it a question about seeing or about doing? (Week 3)

A3. Concept vs. variable. A headline talks about a concept. The data contain a variable.

  1. Give three different variables someone could use to measure “education” in Nepal. Could they give different answers to the same question?
  2. The World Bank variable “women’s college enrollment (% gross)” can be more than 100%. How is that possible? What does that tell you about reading variable names?
  3. Why is a proxy like a descendant in a DAG? (Week 4)

A4. What decided who got X? For each study, name the design (randomized experiment, natural experiment, before/after with a comparison group, or cross-sectional comparison). Then say what decided who got the program.

  1. A municipality had 400 applicants for 200 places in a free coding course, and chose who got in by lottery. Researchers compare the winners and losers a year later.
  2. An NGO compares child health in districts that have its clinics with districts that don’t, using one year of survey data.
  3. Twenty schools started a new maths curriculum in 2023. Twenty similar schools started it in 2025. Researchers compare how scores changed from 2022 to 2024 in both groups.
  4. For each of 60 wards, researchers flipped a coin to decide which wards got new water filters.

Which design has the most back doors to worry about? Why?

A5. “After controlling for…” Each sentence comes from the methods section of a study. Is the control variable a fork, a pipe (mediator), or a collider? Was it a good idea to control for it (or select on it)?

  1. “Clinic visits were linked to better child health, after controlling for household wealth.”
  2. “The fertilizer subsidy raised rice yields. But after controlling for how much fertilizer each farmer used, the effect disappeared.”
  3. “Among students who passed the SEE, those from richer families studied fewer hours.”

A6. Who is it about? Countries with higher internet use have a lower share of young people who are not in school, work, or training.

  1. Can you conclude that a young person in Nepal who starts using the internet is less likely to end up out of school and work? Why or why not?
  2. What is the name for this mistake?
  3. Give one way the country-level pattern could be true even if internet use made no difference to any individual.

A7. True or false? Give a one-sentence reason for each.

  1. If a program’s effect is “not significant,” the program did nothing.
  2. The stars (*, **, ***) in a table tell you how big an effect is.
  3. A well-run randomized experiment in Bihar tells us what would happen in Madhesh.
  4. A program that raises the average score can still make some students worse off.
  5. A small study that finds a large, surprising effect deserves more doubt than a large study that finds a small, expected one.
  6. If a risk “halves,” the absolute change must be large.

Part B: Critique a study

Read this study summary. It is made up. The program, the NGO, and the numbers do not exist.

NoteFictional study: TabletLearn

Headline: “Tablets boost learning: pass rates up 10%.”

The NGO TabletLearn gave every grade 9 student a tablet with maths and science lessons in 30 private schools in Kathmandu Valley. Schools applied to join. After one year, the NGO compared these 30 schools with 30 schools that did not apply.

  • Outcome: a practice exam written by the NGO. 66% of students in tablet schools passed, compared with 60% in the other schools.
  • The NGO says it measured 10 outcomes in total (attendance, homework completion, several subject scores, and so on). The report shows the practice-exam result.
  • Only schools where the tablets still worked at the end of the year were included. Five schools whose tablets broke were dropped.
  • The program cost Rs 12,000 per student.
  • The report includes this table. The outcome is the practice-exam score, in points.
Estimate Std. error
Tablet program 4.0 1.5 **
Student attendance (days) 0.30 0.05 ***
Household income (log) 2.5 0.8 **
Distance to school (km) −0.40 0.30

The NGO concludes: “Every public school in Nepal should get tablets.”

Answer one question at a time.

B1. What exactly is the claim? Is the headline descriptive, predictive, or causal? Which word tells you? Write the estimand the NGO seems to have in mind.

B2. What did they actually measure? The headline says “learning.” What was measured? Give one reason the NGO’s own practice exam might not match “learning.”

B3. How was the data made? What decided which schools got tablets? Draw a DAG with the tablet program, the exam score, and at least one unmeasured cause of both. Name the structure.

B4. What’s in their DAG, and what’s in their model?

  1. The model “controls for” student attendance. Draw an arrow from the tablet program to attendance, if you think it belongs. Is attendance a fork, a pipe, or a collider? What does controlling for it do to the program’s estimate?
  2. Only schools with working tablets were included. Tablets might keep working in well-run schools, and well-run schools might also have better exam results. What structure is this? Which way might it push the result?

B5. Who is it about? Who was studied? Who is the NGO’s conclusion about? List two differences between the two groups that could change the effect.

B6. How big?

  1. The headline says pass rates rose “10%.” What was the change in percentage points? Show how both numbers come from 60% and 66%.
  2. In a school of 1,000 grade 9 students, how many extra students would pass?
  3. Using the cost of Rs 12,000 per student, about how much does each extra passing student cost? (Assume, for now, that the 6-point gap is all caused by the tablets.)

B7. How sure? Use the table.

  1. Write the rough plausible range (estimate ± 2 × std. error) for the tablet program and for distance to school.
  2. Distance to school has no stars. Does that mean distance doesn’t matter? Explain.

B8. How plausible was it before? (Week 1)

  1. Suppose that before seeing this study, you think only 1 in 10 programs like this truly raise exam scores. Studies like this have about 50% power, and use α = 0.05. What is P( true effect | significant result )?
  2. ★ The NGO measured 10 outcomes. Suppose the tablets did nothing for any of them, and each outcome has a 5% chance of a false “significant” result, independently. What is the chance that at least one of the 10 looks significant anyway?

B9. Which of the seven questions was hardest to answer from what the NGO reported? What is the one thing you’d need to know before acting?

Part C: Worked case, bicycles for girls

The lecture discussed Muralidharan & Prakash (2017), Cycling to School. From the slides: Bihar gave girls entering secondary school money for a bicycle, starting in 2006. The finding was that girls’ secondary enrollment rose substantially and the gender gap narrowed. The design was a triple difference: girls vs. boys, Bihar vs. Jharkhand, before vs. after. Use only this information; you don’t need the paper.

C1. Claim. Is the finding descriptive, predictive, or causal? Write it as an estimand using the template from Part 2 of the lecture.

C2. Design. Explain in plain words why each comparison is there.

  1. Why not just compare girls in Bihar before vs. after 2006?
  2. Why also compare girls with boys in Bihar?
  3. Why also compare with Jharkhand?
  4. What has to be true for this design to work? Finish the sentence: “Without the program, the gap between girls and boys in Bihar would have changed…”

C3. ★ A triple difference by hand. These numbers are invented for practice and are not from the paper. They are the share (%) of children enrolled in secondary school.

Before After
Bihar girls 30 45
Bihar boys 50 58
Jharkhand girls 32 40
Jharkhand boys 52 58
  1. How much did enrollment change for Bihar girls? Is that the effect of the program?
  2. Subtract the change for Bihar boys. What do you get?
  3. Do the same for Jharkhand (girls’ change minus boys’ change).
  4. Subtract (c) from (b). This is the triple difference. Compare the three answers in (a), (b) and (d). Why do they shrink?

C4. DAG. Draw a DAG for how the program could raise enrollment. Include at least the program, owning a bicycle, travel time or safety on the way to school, and enrollment. A later study “controls for whether the girl owns a bicycle.” What happens to the estimated effect of the program, and why?

C5. Who. Should Madhesh Province copy the program? List three ways Madhesh might differ from Bihar in 2006 that could make the effect bigger or smaller. Which girls would probably gain the least from a bicycle?

C6. Size and certainty. Before funding it in Madhesh, what would you want the effect stated in? Give the units, and name one alternative you’d compare it to.

C7. Prior plausibility. Was this a surprising, cheap, convenient finding, or a plausible one with a clear mechanism? How should that affect how much you trust it?

C8. Vote again. Would you fund it in Madhesh? Answer yes, no, or “not yet,” and name the one thing you would need to know first.

F2. A one-page critique memo. This is the main assignment for the week. Pick a real report or finding from your own work: a government report, an NGO evaluation, a news story, a consultant’s slide deck.

  • Organize the memo by the seven questions: one short paragraph each.
  • End with a section called “What I’d need to see before acting on this.”
  • Keep it to one page. Short and clear beats long and complete.

Here is a short example, written about a made-up claim, to show the shape:

TipExample memo (fictional claim)

Claim: “Wards with more health posts have fewer child deaths, so every ward should get a second health post.”

  1. Claim. Causal (“so every ward should get”), but the evidence is a comparison between wards.
  2. Measured. “Child deaths” is deaths recorded at the ward office. Wards with more health posts may record deaths more completely, which would hide part of the effect.
  3. Design. Cross-sectional. Health posts were placed by the government, probably in richer or better-connected wards. People didn’t get a health post by coin flip.
  4. DAG. Wealth and road access are forks (they cause both health posts and child survival). The report doesn’t control for either.
  5. Who. Wards in one district. The claim is about every ward in the country.
  6. Size and certainty. The report says “fewer deaths” but gives no number or range.
  7. Prior. It is plausible that health posts help. The open question is how much, and whether a second post adds as much as the first.

What I’d need to see before acting on this: the comparison repeated within wards of similar wealth and road access, the effect stated per 1,000 children with a range, and the cost of a second health post next to the alternatives.

Part G: R challenge (optional)

G1. Write an R function plausible_range(estimate, se) that returns the low and high ends of estimate ± 2 × std. error, and whether the range crosses zero. Use it to check your answers to B7 for all four rows of the TabletLearn table.

G2. ★ Self-selection, simulated. Simulate 1,000 schools. Each school has a motivation drawn from rnorm(). Motivated schools are more likely to join the tablet program: tablet <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(2 * motivation)). The exam score is 50 + 5 * motivation + effect * tablet + rnorm(n, 0, 5).

  1. Set effect <- 0 (the tablets do nothing). Compute the average score of tablet schools minus the average score of other schools. Repeat this 200 times and take the average. What do you get, and why isn’t it 0?
  2. Now give out tablets by coin flip instead: tablet <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.5). What is the difference now?
  3. Repeat (a) and (b) with effect <- 3. Which design recovers the true effect?
  4. Connect this to question 3 of the seven: “What decided who got X?”
