Three building blocks

Fork: a common cause

Pipe: a mediator

Collider: a common effect

  • Every path in every DAG is built from these three pieces.
  • For each, we ask two questions:
    • Does association flow between X and Y through Z?
    • What happens when we hold Z fixed?

What “holding Z fixed” means

People say it many ways. They all mean the same thing:

  • “controlling for Z”
  • “adjusting for Z”
  • “conditioning on Z”
  • “stratifying by Z”

In practice, any of these:

  • Adding Z to a regression (as we did with height last week).
  • Splitting the data into groups of Z and comparing within each group.
  • Selecting your sample by Z: only hospital patients, only published studies, only people with jobs.

That last one is easy to miss. Often the data was “controlled” before you ever saw it.

The fork: a common cause

X ← Z → Y

Does marrying later make you happier?

  • Overall: people who marry later are happier. Within rural or urban areas: no relationship at all.

The fork: what happened?

  • Urban people marry later and (in this made-up world) are happier.
  • Geography is a confounder: a common cause of X and Y.
  • The path A ← G → H is open: it creates a correlation with no causal effect behind it.
  • Holding geography fixed closes the path.

Fork rule

Association flows through a fork. Control for the common cause to close the path.

Do mobile phones lower birth rates?

  • Real World Bank data, 2022. Within income groups the correlation mostly fades (r between -0.31 and -0.06). Wealth drives both phones and smaller families.

You’ve already met forks

Week 2: more bedrooms looked valuable, because bigger houses have more of them.

Week 3: the famous surgeon looked worse, because he got the hardest cases.

  • The classic Simpson’s paradox, where a trend reverses when you split the data, is usually a fork.

More forks

What’s the hidden common cause?

  • Ice cream sales ↔︎ drownings → hot weather
  • Children’s shoe size ↔︎ reading ability → age
  • Firefighters at a fire ↔︎ damage done → size of the fire
  • Carrying a lighter ↔︎ lung cancer → smoking
  • Hospital visits ↔︎ dying → being ill

The pipe: a mediator

X → Z → Y

Is there a gender gap in exam results?

  • A clear gap between groups. But at the same study time, the two lines sit on top of each other: the gap disappears.

The pipe: what happened?

  • In this made-up world, gender affects results only through study time.
  • The path G → T → R is open, and it is a real causal effect.
  • Holding study time fixed closes the path, so the effect vanishes.
  • Does gender matter? Yes, through study time.

Pipe rule

Association flows through a pipe. Holding the mediator fixed blocks the effect. Only do it if you want the direct effect.

Does the anti-fungal treatment work?

  • Overall, treated plants grow more.
  • Compare plants with the same fungus status and the treatment looks useless.
  • Post-treatment bias: never hold fixed something the treatment itself changes, unless that’s the question.

A real paradox: Berkeley admissions, 1973

  • Overall: 45% of men admitted, 30% of women.
  • But in 4 of 6 departments, women were admitted at a higher rate.
  • Women mostly applied to the departments that were hardest to get into.
  • Dot size: number of applicants.

Berkeley: which answer is right?

  • Department is a pipe: sex affects which department you apply to, and departments differ in how hard they are to get into.
  • “Do admissions committees treat women differently?” → hold department fixed (the direct path S → A).
  • “Are women less likely to get in overall?” → don’t (the total effect). Then ask why women ended up applying to the most competitive departments.

The collider: a common effect

X → Z ← Y

Are rich kids lazy?

  • Among people with prestigious jobs, the richer ones seem to work less.
  • But in the whole population (right), wealth and hard work are unrelated.

The collider: what happened?

  • Wealth or hard work can get you the job.
  • If someone has the job but isn’t rich, they probably worked hard. If they didn’t work hard, they’re probably rich.
  • The path W → J ← H is closed by default: no association.
  • Holding J fixed, including by only looking at people with jobs, opens it.

Collider rule

No association flows through a collider, until you hold it fixed. Don’t control for a common effect, and beware of samples selected on one.

Why is published science so often wrong?

  • 200 grant proposals. Newsworthiness and trustworthiness are unrelated.
  • Reviewers fund the top 10% on both combined.
  • Among funded studies: r = -0.77.
  • The most exciting published findings are the least trustworthy, with no bad intent from anyone.
  • Remember Week 1: most published research findings are false.

More colliders

What has been selected on?

  • Restaurants in bad locations serve better food → only the good ones survive
  • Among professional basketball players, height barely predicts success → short players had to be exceptional to make it
  • Among hospital patients, two unrelated diseases look negatively related → either one can put you in hospital (Berkson’s paradox)
  • Among low-birth-weight babies, those of smoking mothers die less often → birth weight is caused by smoking and by other, more dangerous problems
  • Early COVID studies found fewer smokers among hospitalized patients → hospital testing and admission were the collider (Griffith et al., 2020)

The descendant

A variable caused by Z acts like a weaker copy of Z.

Descendants

A proxy for a confounder. We can’t measure wealth exactly, so we used income group. It closes the fork partly, which is why some correlation was left over.

A consequence of a collider. Studying only high earners partly selects on having the job, so it partly opens the collider.

Should I control for it?

Structure Through Z, by default If you hold Z fixed Advice
Fork X ← Z → Y open closed Control for it
Pipe X → Z → Y open closed Only for the direct effect
Collider X → Z ← Y closed open Don’t
Descendant of Z like Z, weaker like Z, weaker Same as Z

The back-door rule

To estimate the effect of X on Y:

  1. List every path between X and Y.
  2. Close every path that starts with an arrow into X (a “back door”).
  3. Don’t hold fixed anything that X causes (if you want the total effect).
  4. Don’t hold fixed a collider, unless you also close the path it opens.

Last week’s examples follow this rule. Height was caused by sex, so we left it out (rule 3). Living area was a back door into bedrooms, so we held it fixed (rule 2).

A paradox inside a paradox

  • Back to Berkeley. What if applicants’ ability affects both which department they apply to and whether they get in?
  • Now department is a pipe and a collider (S → D ← U).
  • Holding department fixed opens S → D ← U → A, so even the “direct effect” is biased.
  • No statistical trick fixes this. We need better data (a measure of ability) or more science.

Part 1: Summary

  • Fork (common cause): creates false associations. Control for it.
  • Pipe (mediator): carries real effects. Controlling for it hides them.
  • Collider (common effect): closed until you control for it, or select on it.
  • Descendants act like weaker copies of their parents.
  • Every paradox this week came from holding the wrong thing fixed, or failing to hold the right thing fixed.

Part 2: Spot the confound

Your turn

For each claim: draw the DAG, name the structure, and decide what to control for.

  1. Students who attend tuition classes score lower on the SEE.
  2. After adjusting for blood pressure, a new drug doesn’t reduce heart attacks.
  3. Among startups that got funding, those with weaker founders had better ideas.
  4. Countries with more doctors per person have more cancer.
  5. People who own more books live longer.
  6. Among people admitted to hospital with COVID, smokers seemed to do better.

Possible answers

  1. Fork: students who are already struggling are the ones sent to tuition. Control for earlier results.
  2. Pipe: the drug works by lowering blood pressure. Don’t adjust if you want the total effect.
  3. Collider: funding needs a strong founder or a strong idea. Don’t trust patterns within a selected sample.
  4. Fork (and a pipe): wealth → more doctors, and wealth → longer lives → more cancer. More doctors also detect more cancer.
  5. Fork: education and income cause both.
  6. Collider: hospitalization. Smoking and severe COVID can each put you there.

There is often more than one defensible DAG. Arguing about which one is right is the science.
